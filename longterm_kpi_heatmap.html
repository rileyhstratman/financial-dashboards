How to read this in 10 seconds. Every cell is a 0–10 rating, read like a stock call: 0–2 Very Bearish · 2–4 Bearish · 4–6 Neutral · 6–8 Bullish · 8–10 Very Bullish. Each rating blends two things: (1) what the ratio's value actually means, scored against published rules of thumb (60% weight), and (2) how it compares to the other three sectors (40% weight). Ratios describing the same category are cross-checked — when they disagree, the cell is flagged "mixed signals." Click any cell for the raw numbers, both score components, and the exact arithmetic. Plain English: instead of making ONE guess about the future, this page rolls the dice 2,000 times per sector and counts the outcomes. "P(up, 3y) = 82%" means: in 82% of the simulated futures, the sector was worth more after 3 years. The quality of the answer depends entirely on the assumed growth (μ) and wobble (σ) — both are listed so you can challenge them.
The machinery. The scope doc's complex-quant sections applied at sector level, with three engines from your notes:
1. GBM Monte Carlo (valuation/profitability/liquidity notes): dVₜ = μVₜ dt + σVₜ dWₜ → V_T = exp[(μ − σ²/2)T + σ√T·Z], Z ~ N(0,1) 2000 simulated 3-yr and 5-yr paths per sector. μ and σ are now measured from 10 years of sector-ETF history (XLE/XLB/XLY/XLF/XLK, to Jun 30 2026); g remains an assumption. ⚠ Caveat: using a trailing 10-yr return as forward drift assumes the next decade looks like the last — most aggressive for Info Tech (25.6%/yr was an exceptional decade).
2. Vasicek rate simulation (valuation/liquidity notes): drₜ = a(b − rₜ)dt + σᵣ dWₜ with r₀=4.0%, a=0.30, b=3.5%, σᵣ=1.0% Simulated monthly for 5 yrs → the mean terminal rate feeds the discount rate below.
3. Growing perpetuity PV (simple-valuation + generating-earnings notes): PV per $1 of current cash flow = 1 / (r − g), r = Vasicek mean rate + 4% equity risk premium
Seeded RNG — numbers are identical every time you open this file, so anything you question can be traced. μ and σ are measured from 10-yr sector-ETF history (backward-looking — past ≠ future); g is still an assumption bounded by the scope doc's 2–4% rule.

Scoring — how every 0–10 rating is built

Step 1 — Absolute score (60% weight). Each ratio's value is scored 0–10 against published rule-of-thumb anchors by straight-line interpolation. This captures what the number means on its own. Percentile grounding (added Jul 14): for the three valuation ratios (trailing P/E, forward P/E, PEG) the absolute score is now a 50/50 blend of the anchor score and the sector's own-history cheapness percentile (its position within its own Jan-2004→Jan-2025 Damodaran values, 22 vintages) — motivated by the outcome backtest, which found what predictive signal exists lives in own-history deviations, not cross-sector levels (though the extended 90-obs backtest shows the valuation signal is modest: p=.048, and regime-dependent). Defense is excluded from the blend (its history is the old industry aggregate; its current values are the 10-major company set — the bases don't mix). Crucially, not every ratio is "lower = better" or "higher = better" — three have a sweet spot where both extremes are penalized:

Step 2 — Relative score (40% weight). Computed in goodness space: each sector's ABSOLUTE score (not its raw ratio) is min–max ranked 0–10 across the 4 sectors. This is what makes cross-sector comparison correct for sweet-spot metrics — a sector near the P/E sweet spot beats both the distress-cheap one and the expensive one, which raw-value ranking would get wrong.
Step 3 — Blend & weight. KPI rating = 0.6 × absolute + 0.4 × relative (− DuPont penalty for ROE; − 1.5 peak-earnings penalty when net margin or ROE sits at ≥90th percentile of the sector's own 2004–25 history, i.e. roughly the top two observations in two decades — the backtest's most robust finding: peak levels mean-revert, FE p=.014, pooled p=.03, 2-yr p=.014 on 90 obs). Category rating = plain average of its KPI ratings (N/A excluded). Overall = weighted average of seven category ratings (Jul 14: Cash Flow added, carving 2.5 pts each from Profitability, Valuation, Efficiency and Liquidity — the categories it partially overlaps): Profitability 20% · Valuation 20% · Efficiency 15% · Growth 12.5% · Leverage 12.5% · Liquidity 10% · Cash Flow 10%. No universal weighting standard exists (Altman Z, CFI etc. choose purpose-specific weights); these are documented and deliberately adjustable, renormalized when a category is N/A. Membership: Profitability = margins (gross/net/EBITDA) · Liquidity = current + quick · Efficiency = turnovers + ROA · Growth = expected EPS + delivered + expected revenue + sustainable growth · Cash Flow = cash-generation margin + CapEx/Depreciation · Valuation = forward P/E + trailing P/E + PEG · Leverage = market D/E + book D/E + ROE.
Step 4 — Cross-checks between ratios. Ratios are tied together by accounting identities, and the panel reads them against each other: net÷gross margin (how much gross profit survives operating costs), ROE÷ROA (DuPont leverage multiplier), P/E÷measured growth (PEG-style: is the price backed by history?). Ratings within a category that disagree by >2 points flag ⚠ mixed signals.
Verdict bands: 0–2 Very Bearish · 2–4 Bearish · 4–6 Neutral · 6–8 Bullish · 8–10 Very Bullish.

Interpretation warnings (read once)

· The anchors are judgment calls — standard finance rules of thumb, but not laws. They're published in the table above precisely so you can challenge them. Softened Jul 14: the three valuation ratios are now half-grounded in each sector's own measured history (percentile blend), and margins/ROE carry a backtest-measured peak penalty — the judgment-call share of the scoring is shrinking as grounding lands.
· Own-history percentiles inherit their window's regime — now diluted. Windows extended Jul 14 from 8 vintages (2018–25) to 22 (2004–25), spanning the GFC, the 2013 gold crash, the 2015–16 oil bust, COVID, and 2022. The 2021 bubble multiples (IT fwd P/E 156×) are now 1 observation of 22 rather than 1 of 8. Residual honesty: the extended backtest also showed the valuation signal is weaker than the 2018–25 window suggested (p=.048 on 90 obs; insignificant in 2008–17 alone), while margin mean-reversion held up everywhere — the peak-earnings penalty is the better-grounded of the two mechanisms.
· P/E scored lower-is-better = a value lens. Under a growth lens a high P/E can be justified; the 2–5 yr thesis step decides that per company. Defense's P/E (35.5×) is now measured from ten real companies — the growth-lens debate is real there, not a data artifact (the old 79-firm aggregate's 45.9× was half loss-making firms).
· EPS is displayed but NOT rated — raw EPS isn't comparable across sectors (share counts differ arbitrarily).
· US Defense & Aerospace = ten US-listed majors + emerging watchlist. Broad aerospace by construction (GE, Howmet, TransDigm, HEICO are commercial-aero-heavy); international primes (BAE, Rheinmetall...) are not in the dataset. Drone/space up-and-comers (KTOS, AVAV, RKLB) are tracked unrated on the Sector Profiles tab.
· Precious Metals' Very Bullish rating is a cyclical-peak snapshot. With gold near $4,000, miners show fat margins, high ROA and a cheap PEG — which is exactly how commodity producers look at the TOP of their cycle. Ratio frameworks systematically over-rate cyclicals at peak earnings ("peak-earnings trap"): the low multiple prices in the fall. The 2008–2025 backtest (data-source table, 90 obs) backs this empirically — its single most robust finding: margins fat vs a sector's own history predicted LOWER forward returns (FE p=.014, pooled p=.03, 2-yr p=.014), and this sector's net margin and ROE both sit near two-decade highs (−1.5 peak penalties applied). Read this row with the commodity cycle in mind.
· These are sector averages — a Bullish sector can contain terrible companies and vice versa. Company selection is the next pipeline stage.

Outcome backtest (grounding option 2)Archives + Yahoo Finance ETF annual total returns, Jul 14 2026Did these ratings predict forward sector returns? Panel: 5 sectors × 18 Jan-vintages (2008–2025, extended same day from the original 8) scored with this dashboard's own anchors (archive-available subset: valuation, net margin, ROE, expected growth) vs next-calendar-year ETF total returns (XLE/XLB/GDX/ITA/XLK). n=90, spanning the GFC, 2013 gold crash, 2015–16 oil bust, COVID, 2022, and 2025. Finding 1 — cross-sector levels did NOT predict: the composite's rank correlation with forward returns averaged −0.03 (positive in 7 of 18 years — a coin flip). Finding 2 — margin mean-reversion is the robust within-sector signal: fat margins vs a sector's own history predicted LOWER next-year returns (sector-FE −11.8 per SD, p=.014; also significant in pooled levels p=.03 and at 2-yr p=.014; sorts: 6.4% avg forward return after high-margin years vs 18.9% after low). Consequences: (1) the peak-earnings penalty is the best-grounded mechanism in the scoring; (2) the valuation percentile blend is kept but should be read as modest tilt, not alpha; (3) the 40% relative (cross-sector) component is descriptive, not predictive; (4) regression-fitted weights are NOT adopted — pooled coefficients (except margins) are insignificant, so the documented midpoint weights stand. Complex-layer columns (page 2)

μ, σ, g: assumed inputs (drift, volatility, perpetual growth) — shown so you can challenge them.
P(up, 3y): share of 2000 GBM paths ending above start after 3 yrs.
P(CAGR > 7%, 5y): share of paths whose 5-yr multiple beats 1.07⁵ ≈ 1.403 — a hurdle-rate test.
Median 5y multiple: the middle simulated outcome; ×1.50 = +50% over 5 yrs.
PV per $1 (r−g): growing-perpetuity value of $1 of current cash flow — bigger = long-duration cash flows worth more if g materializes. Blows up as g → r; that fragility is the lesson, not a bug.
Sector: Radar = category ratings (0–10 bullish/bearish scale). Strips = where each raw KPI sits inside the 4-sector range, with its rating and verdict.
Interactive full DCF — a DCF answers one question: "what is a company worth today, given the cash we think it will generate in the future?" Money later is worth less than money now, so each future year's cash gets shrunk ("discounted") back to today. This is the 4-step model from the scope doc, live: change any assumption and every intermediate number recomputes. Defaults are generic illustrations, not any real company.

What am I looking at? (30 seconds)
This grid answers one question: "how much is $1 per year of growing cash flow worth today?" — under every combination of two assumptions you have to make in any long-term valuation:

· Rows (r) = the discount rate — the yearly return you demand for waiting and taking risk. Higher r = you're more impatient/risk-averse = future cash is worth less to you.
· Columns (g) = the growth assumption — how fast you believe that $1 grows forever. Higher g = more optimism.

How to use it: pick a row and slide your finger right. Same company, same cash — only your optimism changes. Notice the value doesn't rise smoothly, it accelerates: at r = 8%, going from g = 2% to g = 4% adds +50% of "value" from a 2-point assumption change and nothing real. That acceleration is the whole lesson: long-term valuations are hostage to the r − g gap.

· Green cells = high value, red = low — the color follows the number, not "good/bad".
· ⚠ cells = g is nearly equal to r. The formula divides by ~zero and spits out infinity-ish numbers. If a valuation you read lives in that corner, the assumptions are doing all the work.
· Hover any cell to see its exact arithmetic.

Why you should care: the DCF's terminal value (tab 4) uses this exact formula, and it's usually 60–80% of a company's total valuation. When two analysts disagree wildly on a price target, the disagreement almost always lives in this grid — in which cell they chose.
The r − g fragility grid. Value of $1/yr of growing cash flow: PV = 1 / (r − g). This is the single most important intuition for 2–5 yr valuation: the closer growth gets to the discount rate, the more explosive — and fragile — the valuation. A 1-point change in either assumption can double or halve "intrinsic value." That's why the scope doc caps g at 2–4% and why terminal value dominates every DCF.
Read a row: hold your discount rate fixed, walk right as growth assumptions get more optimistic, and watch value inflate nonlinearly. Cells where g approaches r are marked unstable — the perpetuity formula breaks down (division by ~zero), which in practice means "your assumptions, not math, are producing the number."

The buyback problem: who can actually fund their promises?

Sustainable growth (classic) = ROE × (1 − dividend payout). But in the buyback era dividends understate distributions: counting buybacks, three of five sectors return MORE than 100% of net income to shareholders — their cash-adjusted sustainable growth is NEGATIVE, meaning any real growth must be financed by new debt, equity issuance, or margin expansion, not retained earnings. Why this page exists. Everything else in this terminal is about picking what to own. This page is about not losing it: the asymmetry of losses, sizing positions so no single mistake is fatal, why the ORDER of returns matters once money is being withdrawn, inflation's quiet tax, and the actual tax bill. Educational tooling — not investment, tax, or legal advice.

1 · The mathematics of loss and recovery

Gain needed to recover = Loss ÷ (1 − Loss)
A loss and its recovery are NOT symmetric — the recovery is computed on the smaller base. This is the core argument for capping drawdowns.
Fixed reference points: −10% → +11.1% · −20% → +25% · −33.3% → +50% · −50% → +100% · −90% → +900%. The curve is convex: each extra point of loss costs more than the last. (Sources: Shell Capital "Math of Loss"; Tsai Capital.)

2 · Position sizing (predefined risk limit)

Position size (shares) = (Total capital × Risk %) ÷ (Entry price − Stop-loss price)
The risk % caps the LOSS if the stop is hit — not the position value. Your example: $10,000 × 1% = $100 max loss; stop $2 below entry → 50 shares. Gaps and slippage can push the real loss past the stop — this is a limit on plan, not on reality.

3 · Sequence-of-returns risk (why ORDER matters when withdrawing)

Two portfolios earn the same ten yearly returns — one gets the bad years first, the other gets them last. Without withdrawals they end identical (multiplication commutes). With withdrawals they do not: selling into a drawdown converts temporary losses into permanent ones.
Return sequence used (same set, opposite order): −15%, −10%, +5%, +12%, +8%, +15%, +10%, +7%, +9%, +11%. Arithmetic mean 5.2%/yr in both cases; the geometric mean (what compounding actually pays) is lower — volatility drag, σ²/2, the same term as in the GBM tab. This asymmetry is why distribution-phase portfolios hold stabler assets than accumulation-phase ones (CFA Institute, capital preservation & wealth). Not a recommendation — a mechanism.

4 · Inflation-adjusted (real) return

Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
The common shortcut (nominal − inflation) overstates the real return slightly; the exact division matters over long horizons. "Preserving capital" nominally while losing purchasing power is a hidden loss (Investopedia, preservation of capital).

5 · Tax efficiency — three measures

Future tax liability — Potential Capital Gains Exposure: PCGE = Unrealized capital gains ÷ Total net assets
Positive PCGE = embedded gains that can be distributed as taxable capital gains even to NEW holders of a fund; negative = harvestable losses (Morningstar methodology).
⚠ Formula correction (flagged during intake): the formula supplied as "TER = (1 − (1+after-tax)/(1+pre-tax)) × 100%" is actually Morningstar's Tax-Cost Ratio. The standard Tax Efficiency Ratio is simply after-tax ÷ pre-tax × 100%. Both are computed above, correctly labeled, plus the simple-difference variant. (Sources: Morningstar tax-cost methodology; AnalystPrep CFA-III post-tax returns; Passiv.)
